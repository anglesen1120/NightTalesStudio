The Call Before the Coastal Road Crashes

I read the case beside a dark coastal road. The records place two crashes on the same stretch, ten years apart. Neither scene contains a brake mark.

According to the comparison in the records, both crashes happened at night. The weather was clear each time. The road surface was dry, and visibility was reported as good.

The first report describes one car leaving the coastal stretch. The second report describes another car leaving it years later. In both accounts, the driver was alone.

The records place the crashes ten years apart. They also identify the drivers as strangers. They lived in different provinces and had no recorded connection.

The first investigation noted the dry conditions. It noted the clear view ahead. It also recorded that the road held no visible brake marks near the crash site.

The later report described nearly the same approach. Good visibility was recorded again. The road surface was dry again. Investigators found no brake marks there either.

Neither driver could be interviewed afterwards, according to the reports. That left the final minutes inside each car without a direct account. Only the recorded evidence remained.

The files gave no social link between the drivers. One lived in a different province from the other. Their paths met only in the comparison of two reports.

The later record also notes that the road had been resurfaced once between the crashes. The second investigation did not mention that change. The omission would matter later.

Phone records supplied the first shared detail. Each driver had taken an incoming call shortly before the crash. Both calls came from the same phone number.

The records do not describe the words exchanged. They place both drivers on that incoming call before their cars left the road. The timing appears in both investigations.

The comparison now held three matching points. The same coastal stretch. No brake marks. And a call before each crash, separated by ten years.

The drivers were alone, according to the reports. They did not know each other. Yet the call records attached the same number to both final journeys.

A new investigator reopened the comparison while digitising older files. The investigator noticed the shared number first. Then the resurfacing omission appeared in the later record.

That omission weakened the most obvious physical comparison. The road had not looked exactly the same across the decade. Any missing mark could have vanished with the old surface.

The absence of brake marks still remained in both reports. But the later road had changed between investigations. The files could not treat that absence as identical evidence.

The shared call became the stronger connection. The investigator had found it in records, not at either scene. The number linked the journeys, but not the reason for them.

The records do not identify who used the number. They do not preserve what was said on either call.

Nothing in the comparison proves the drivers were targeted. Nothing explains why two strangers received the same call before reaching the same stretch of road.

The new investigator placed the two records together again. The comparison corrected one physical assumption. It did not close the unanswered part of the case.

The old surface was gone. The later road had been changed. Whatever the missing marks once meant could no longer be checked against the ground.

The number remained the cleanest link between the two files. It appeared before each crash, then disappeared back into the silence of the road.

The caller remains unidentified in the recorded account. The road keeps its silence between passing lights. What do you think happened before those two crashes?
