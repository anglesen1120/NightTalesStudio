The Number Before the Crashes

I read two crash reports from the same coastal road. They were written ten years apart, on clear nights, yet both mention an incoming call just before the cars left the road. The reports never identify who made it.

According to the reports, both crashes happened at night. The weather was clear and dry. Visibility was recorded as good. The road section was the same in both cases. Ten years separated the two events.

The reports describe one driver in each car. Neither driver had a passenger. The drivers did not know each other. They lived in different provinces. Neither could be interviewed afterward.

The first report describes an ordinary night on the coast. There was no recorded storm. The road was dry. The surrounding stretch offered few signs of activity. Distant headlights were the only movement noted in the scene.

One detail stood out in the first report. Investigators found no brake marks near the crash site. The road surface showed no sign that the driver had tried to slow before leaving it.

A decade passed before the second crash. Between the two events, the coastal stretch was resurfaced once. The later investigation file records no discussion of that change.

The second report again describes clear weather and good visibility. It also records no brake marks. The same absence appeared at the second site, despite the years between the crashes.

The circumstances also repeated in quieter ways. The second driver was alone. That driver had no known connection to the first. The reports place the two people in separate provinces and separate lives.

The first important clue came from the phone records. They place the first driver on an incoming call shortly before the crash. The record does not preserve what was said during that call.

The second phone record contains the same kind of entry. An incoming call arrived shortly before the second crash. According to the record, it came from the same phone number.

The shared number was not a detail from memory. It appeared in both sets of phone records. Each call came before its corresponding crash. The timing was close enough to bring the files together.

Another detail complicated the comparison. The road had been resurfaced between the crashes. The second file does not mention that work. That omission made the two locations harder to compare by surface evidence alone.

According to the later account, a new investigator noticed the number while digitising old files. The investigator reopened the comparison. The connection was not found at either original scene.

The early investigations had treated the crashes as separate events. The resurfacing was absent from the second file. The shared number appeared only after the older records were compared again.

That later comparison changed the shape of the case. Two unrelated drivers had taken calls from the same number. Both crashed on the same road section. Neither report explains why the calls mattered.

The records establish the calls, but not the caller. They do not show what either driver heard. They do not establish why both cars left the road without brake marks.

The new investigator could reopen the comparison. That did not reopen either driver's account. Both remained unreachable, and the two original reports still contained their separate conclusions.

The drivers left no shared history behind. The reports say they did not know each other. Their lives began in different provinces, then crossed only in records made after the crashes.

The road itself had changed between the two nights. Its surface was replaced once. The absence of brake marks remained in both accounts, but the later file did not preserve every detail needed to place that absence in context.

The comparison could connect the two crashes without explaining them. It could identify a pattern without identifying the caller. The road kept its silence, and the number remained only a line in two separate records. What do you think happened before those calls?
