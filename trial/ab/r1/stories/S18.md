The Bowl That Faced West

Each morning, I found the incense bowl facing the window. That window looked toward the house we had sold. I had promised my grandmother something before we left, and I had forgotten what it was.

We moved in during a week of cold rain. The house smelled of new wood and unopened boxes. My mother chose the smallest upstairs room for the family altar. She placed the bowl in the centre. The window faced west, toward the old neighbourhood.

The first night, the house made ordinary sounds. Boards settled. Pipes clicked behind the walls. By morning, the rooms had gone silent. Even the road outside seemed distant. At my neck, the cold lingered after I left the altar room.

I blamed the bowl's shape at first. Its base was rounded from years of use. I set it against the back rail. Then I placed a folded cloth beneath it. The next morning, the cloth lay flat, and the bowl faced the window again.

My mother noticed on the third morning. She did not touch it. She only closed the curtains and went downstairs. When I returned after breakfast, the curtains were still closed. The bowl had turned beneath them.

We tested the floor with a cup and a marble. Nothing rolled toward the window. The shelf stood level. I moved the bowl to a low table near the door. Before sunset, it remained there. Before dawn, it faced west once more.

That evening, I opened the last box from the old house. Under winter clothes, I found a strip of faded cloth and a small wooden spoon. The spoon belonged beside the old altar. I remembered my grandmother washing it before every offering.

Her final week had been quiet. She had asked me to sit beside her at the old altar. When we moved, she wanted one bowl of rice left there each morning for seven days. I promised. I remember saying yes.

The move had lasted two days. My grandmother was gone by then. My mother packed the altar before sunrise. The old house went to its new owners that afternoon. We never returned for the rice.

I told myself the promise had belonged to another life. It had been made in a house we no longer owned. Still, the bowl kept facing west. On the seventh morning, I counted the empty days on my fingers.

The bowl had not moved closer to the window. It had turned toward the old house. A thin crack crossed its rim. Inside it lay a single grain of rice, though nobody had placed food there.

I searched through our packing notes. The altar cloth was missing. So was the wooden spoon. Both had stayed in the old house, packed inside a cupboard we had forgotten. The promise had not followed us. I had.

My mother asked me to leave it alone. Her voice stayed low. She said the old house had new owners. We had no right to enter. I agreed, then took the spoon from the box and put it in my coat.

I went there before breakfast. The old house stood behind its new fence. No lights showed inside. The gate was unlocked. I carried the spoon, the cloth, and a bowl of rice from our kitchen.

I left everything on the old doorstep. The rice sat in the bowl. The cloth covered the spoon. I spoke my promise once, then stepped away. The west-facing window remained dark.

At home, the bowl was still turned toward the window. I waited beside it until the sunlight reached the floor. Then the ceramic gave a small sound and split along the old crack.

The price was mine to pay. I had kept the bowl because it was the last object my grandmother touched. Keeping it meant keeping the promise unfinished. Returning it meant losing that piece of her.

My mother wrapped the broken bowl in plain paper. She did not throw it away. We placed it on the highest shelf, where nobody could use it. The altar remained without a vessel.

For the next seven mornings, I walked back to the old house. I left one bowl of rice each time. The gate stayed open. The house stayed silent. On the last morning, the spoon was gone.

I never learned who took it. The new owners did not answer when I knocked. I left the cloth folded on the step and returned home through the rain. Behind me, no door opened.

After that, the bowl never turned. The cold at my neck faded. My mother began using the upstairs room for folded blankets. We did not rebuild the altar. The west-facing window remained covered each morning.

The bowl stayed beside the window after that. It never turned again. My mother stopped asking about the missing shelf. We kept the house, but the old one remained part of our mornings. Some promises end when they are kept. What would you have given back?
