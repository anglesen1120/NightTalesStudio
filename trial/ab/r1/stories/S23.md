The Keeper's Last Entry

I kept the lamp in a room above the waves. Each night, I wrote down when it turned itself on. The fuse had been pulled for a week.

I was the keeper of a small lighthouse on the northern coast. The tower stood beyond a stretch of black rock. Salt stayed on the windows, even after the rain stopped. My work was simple. I checked the lantern, trimmed the wick, and recorded every fault in a clothbound log.

The lamp had been unreliable since I arrived. It gave one dull flash after midnight. Then it went dark again. I blamed damp wiring and old fittings. The previous keeper had warned me about the weather. He had not mentioned the lamp.

I began writing down the nights it happened. The first entry took only one line. Lamp lit at half past one. Fuse intact. The next morning, the bulb was cold. There was no smell of smoke. The switch rested exactly where I had left it.

The sound outside changed whenever the lamp came on. Waves struck the rocks below the tower. Then their noise thinned away. It faded until the room seemed sealed from the sea. I could still see the water through the glass. I simply could not hear it.

I replaced the fuse twice. I checked every wire with a meter. Nothing carried power when the lamp was dark. On the third night, I removed the fuse and carried it downstairs. I locked it in a tin box beside my bed.

The lamp came on before dawn. I heard the click above me. The sound passed through the ceiling and into the bed frame. I opened the tin box. The fuse was still inside. Water had gathered beneath it, though the room was dry.

I searched the keeper's cupboard for older records. Behind a row of cracked mugs, I found another log. Its cover had swollen from damp. The pages stopped halfway through winter. Every entry described the same silence after the lamp lit.

The final entry in that older log was written in a narrow hand. It said the lamp was not drawing power. It was answering a signal. Beneath that line, someone had written, Do not let it see the shore.

I thought the line meant the lamp should not face the water. The lantern room had three windows, all fixed in place. I covered the largest one with a canvas sheet. That night, the light appeared behind the cloth.

The glow was weak at first. It pulsed through the canvas like a hand passing behind it. I pulled the sheet down. Beyond the glass, the sea was black and flat. Something pale stood far out on the water, where the waves should have been.

I could not make out a face or a body. I saw only a vertical shape, steady against the dark. The lamp brightened once. The shape moved closer without disturbing the water. Then the sound of the sea began fading again.

I ran downstairs and opened my log. The page had been left blank when I checked it earlier. Now a fresh line crossed the paper. It was not in my handwriting. The ink was wet, and the words were simple. You have kept it waiting.

The lamp went dark. In that instant, the shape stood on the balcony outside the lantern room. The door behind me opened by itself. On the last page, another sentence appeared beneath the first. The keeper must finish the record.

I never touched the balcony door. I went down the stairs with the log under my coat. Every step gave a soft wooden knock. Above me, the lamp came on again. The glow followed me through the tower, shining beneath each closed door.

At the entrance, I found wet marks across the floor. They began at the threshold and ended beside the fuse box. The marks were narrow and uneven. None led back outside. The sea remained silent beyond the walls.

I opened the fuse box. The tin box was inside it. I had left it beside my bed. The fuse rested in the box, covered in salt. A faint warmth came from the metal door.

I took the log and left before sunrise. The path down the rocks was slick with rain. Behind me, the lantern kept turning. Its beam crossed the water and returned. Each time it passed, the waves sounded farther away.

At the foot of the hill, I stopped beside an old marker stone. The lighthouse stood above me, dark except for its lamp. I looked down at the log. The last page was filled now. The writing had changed.

The new lines described the path I had taken. They described the marker stone. They even described the coat around my shoulders. The final line had not been written yet. I closed the book and walked toward the road.

I left the log beneath the lamp and walked toward the shore. Behind me, the glass chimney clicked as it cooled. The sea had gone quiet enough to hear the pages turning. I did not look back. I still have the key to that lighthouse, though I have never found the door it opens. Have you ever heard something like this?
