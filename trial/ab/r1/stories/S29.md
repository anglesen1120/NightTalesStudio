The Key She Left Behind

For ten years, I promised my mother I would take her home. She died before I kept that promise. Since then, I dream of our village house with every light burning, while its key stays in my pocket.

I had carried that key through every rented room. It was small, darkened with age, and warm from my hand. My mother kept it beneath her pillow after we moved away. She said the village house was waiting for us. I told her we would return when work became easier.

Work became a chain of ordinary excuses. One project ended, and another began. Rent rose. Trains ran late. My mother stopped asking for a date. She only folded our old village clothes into a wooden trunk. Each time I saw it, I promised again.

The city apartment was always warm at night. That warmth came from the kitchen, the pipes, and the traffic below. Still, it carried the smell of burnt incense. My mother burned one stick before sleep. She said the scent reminded her of the house.

The first dream came during my third year in the city. I stood outside the village house. Its windows glowed behind the trees. The door was unlocked. Inside, I heard a chair move across the floor. Then my mother called my childhood name.

I woke with the key pressed into my palm. My mother was asleep in the next room. Incense smoke drifted beneath her door. I almost drove back that morning. Instead, my manager called before sunrise. I stayed, and the dream returned the following night.

Years earlier, my mother had placed the key in my hand. Her fingers were thin, but her grip was firm. She made me promise to unlock the house before she died. I laughed then. I told her the house had waited this long.

She did not laugh with me. She asked me to say the promise again. I said I would take her back. I said we would sleep beneath the old ceiling. Then I put the key in my pocket and went to work.

I forgot the exact words by the next week. My mother did not. She kept the trunk ready near the door. Whenever I postponed the trip, she moved it back against the wall. The house stayed in my dreams. The key stayed with me.

After ten years, my mother died in the city. We held the quiet funeral near the apartment. That night, the village house appeared again. Every window was lit. The front door stood open. My mother waited inside, but she did not come toward me.

She stood beside the old staircase. Her hands were empty. Behind her, the rooms looked warm and ready. I tried to cross the threshold. The floorboards answered with three slow knocks. My mother pointed toward my coat pocket.

I pulled out the key. It had become hot enough to hurt. My mother turned toward the back room. The incense smell thickened around us. Then the lights began going out, one room at a time. She remained beside the last lamp.

At dawn, I woke in my city room. The key lay on the floor beside my bed. My mother's old trunk stood open. Inside was the village house deed and one folded shawl. The promise had found its way back to me.

I drove to the village that same morning. The road had narrowed beneath the trees. The house looked smaller than I remembered. No light showed in the windows. When I unlocked the door, the smell of burnt incense came from the back room.

Nothing moved inside. Dust covered the table. A chair faced the doorway. Beside it sat a bowl with no food in it. I opened every window. The warm air left slowly, carrying the incense scent into the yard.

I spent the night on the floor because the bed felt borrowed. Near midnight, the hallway creaked. I waited for my mother to call me. The sound came again, farther away this time. It stopped beside the locked back room.

The next morning, I found that room open. I had not touched it. My mother's trunk stood inside, exactly as she had left it. The folded clothes were gone. In their place lay a small brass lamp, still warm.

I carried the lamp to the front room. It lit the chair and the wall above it. There was no flame inside. I sat until evening. When darkness came, the lamp went out by itself.

I did not sell the house. I could not leave it locked again. Each week, I repaired one small thing. I fixed the gate. I cleared the roof. I washed the windows that had held those lights in my dreams.

For the first month, I slept there alone. The dreams stopped. So did the incense smell. On the first morning without either, I found the key on the table. It was cold for the first time in ten years.

The next morning, I left the city for good. I found work near the village. I kept the house open, though nobody else lived there. Each month, I carried rice to the back room. I lit one stick of incense. The smoke always bent toward the empty chair. My mother had waited ten years. I had waited for a better time. What would you have done?
