The Attendance List

I was the caretaker of an empty school when the blackboard began writing back. Every evening, classroom seven carried the smell of wet chalk, though nobody had entered it for years.

The school had been closed since the roof started leaking. Most windows were boarded from inside. I kept one key ring and a narrow list of jobs. Lock the gates. Check the pipes. Walk the corridors before leaving.

I worked there alone each evening. The building settled around me. Pipes clicked behind the walls. Old radiators gave out small knocks. Those sounds belonged to the place, so I never counted them as company.

The corridor outside classroom seven had one working light. It flickered whenever the wind pressed against the west windows. I replaced the bulb twice. Each time, it worked until I reached the far stairwell.

Classroom seven stayed locked because its ceiling had begun to sag. I checked the handle during every round. Dust lay thick beneath the door. No footprints crossed it, not even mine.

The teacher assigned there had vanished during the final term. I knew that much from an old staff photograph. Her name was written beneath the picture. The ink had faded, but the letters remained clear.

The caretaker before me had left one warning in the maintenance ledger. He wrote that classroom seven should stay closed after sunset. There was no reason beside it. The next page had been torn out.

One wet evening, I found the classroom door open. The corridor light blinked above me. Inside, the blackboard held the teacher’s name in large white letters. The chalk lines shone as though they had just been drawn.

I checked every window first. They were sealed. The only chalk in the room sat inside a locked cupboard. I unlocked it with the master key. The sticks were dry and untouched.

I wiped the name away with a damp cloth. The board squeaked beneath my hand. When I finished, the cloth was warm. The chalk dust on it had turned to a thin paste.

I locked the room and waited in the corridor. Nothing moved inside. Still, the handle pressed down once. It rose slowly, then settled. I heard three soft taps against the blackboard.

The following evening, the name was back. This time, a second line stood beneath it. It read, Please take attendance. I had never seen those words in the old records.

I brought the ledger with me. The last attendance page listed only one teacher. Her name appeared beside the final evening class. The column for the students was blank.

I read the name aloud without meaning to. The corridor light went out. From inside the classroom came the scrape of a chair. Then another. Then many more, moving across the floor.

When the light returned, every desk faced the blackboard. The teacher’s name had been wiped clean. In its place, one line of chalk waited for me. It said, Present.

I left the ledger on the floor and backed away. The door closed before I reached it. Through the wood, I heard a woman turn pages. She stopped at the blank student column.

I did not touch the handle. The light above me flickered in a steady rhythm. Each blink sounded like a small knock. Behind the door, chalk moved across the board.

The writing stopped when the front gate alarm rang. I ran outside and left the building unlocked. From the pavement, I watched the corridor light burn through the boarded windows.

The next morning, I returned with the district manager. Classroom seven was locked again. Dust covered the floor. The ledger lay where I had dropped it, but the final page was no longer blank.

A new entry filled the page. It listed the teacher’s name, then mine. Beside both names, the same word appeared. Present. The ink looked old, though I had never written it.

I handed in my keys that afternoon. The district manager asked why. I told him the pipes were making noises. I did not mention the door, the desks, or the page.

The school has stayed closed since then. I pass it only in daylight. Sometimes classroom seven shows a pale square through the boards. Sometimes the corridor light flickers beneath the door.

I stopped wiping the board after that. The name still appears, but only when the corridor light begins to flicker. I leave the room untouched now. By morning, the chalk is always dry. The rest of the school remains silent. Have you ever heard something like this?
