The Key I Carried Home

For ten years, I dreamed of our village house with every light burning. The key stayed in my pocket. My mother had asked me to take her home, and I kept telling myself there would be time.

I worked in the city above a narrow street. My days began before sunrise. My nights ended beside a window full of traffic. In the drawer beneath my clothes, I kept the old iron key. Its teeth were worn smooth from years of being carried.

My mother kept one room for village things. There were folded blankets, dried herbs, and a cracked clay lamp. She never asked me loudly. She only touched the key and asked when we would leave. I always answered, soon.

Then work found reasons to keep me there. A new contract came first. Then a manager left. Then the rent rose. Each reason had a shape I could hold. Together, they became ten years.

My mother grew quieter during that time. She stopped folding clothes for the journey. She stopped correcting my promises. On the last winter evening I remember clearly, she placed the key in my palm and closed my fingers around it.

The room smelled of burnt incense. A thin grey trail climbed toward the ceiling. My mother said the village house disliked being left dark. Then she smiled, as though she had made a small joke. I promised again.

She died before spring came. I handled the arrangements in the city. I took leave from work for three days. After that, I returned to the same apartment and put the key back in the drawer.

That night, the dream began. I stood outside the village house. Every window was lit. Smoke drifted from the little room at the back. From inside came my mother's voice, asking whether I had brought the key.

The dream returned each night. The road was always empty. The house was always warm. I never saw my mother. I only heard the latch lift, followed by the soft scrape of a chair across the floor.

One night, the dream changed. The voice did not ask about the key. It asked why I had promised to come back if I meant to stay away. I woke with ash on my sleeve, though the incense bowl was cold.

I left for the village the next morning. The drive took most of the day. I held the key while I drove. At dusk, I reached the old road and found the gate unlatched.

The house stood exactly as I remembered it. Dust covered the steps. Vines crossed the wall. Yet a lamp burned behind the front window. I used the key, and the door opened before I turned it.

Inside, the air held burnt incense and warm dust. A bowl sat on the table. Beside it lay my mother's folded shawl. I reached for the cloth. From the back room came the scrape of a chair.

I carried the lamp into the back room. My mother's chair faced the open doorway. No one sat there. On the wall, the shadow of the chair stretched toward me, though the lamp stood behind it.

The lamp went out. In the darkness, my mother spoke once. She said the house had waited because I had asked it to. Then the key grew hot in my hand, and the front door closed.

I did not run. I put the key on the table and opened every window. The house breathed out its old smell. By dawn, the lamps were dark. The shawl had vanished from the chair.

I stayed until noon. I swept the floors. I washed the bowl. In the back room, I found a stack of unpaid village bills beneath the chair. The oldest one carried my mother's handwriting.

The bills were for repairs, taxes, and the lamp oil she had bought each month. She had kept the house ready for our return. I paid everything at the village office before sunset.

When I returned, the key was no longer warm. I placed it beside the bowl and slept on the floor. For the first time in ten years, I dreamed of my mother without the house behind her.

The next morning, I locked the door. I left the key in the lock for a moment, then removed it. The house looked smaller than it had in my dreams. Its windows held only the pale morning.

I visit the house every month now. I clean the room, replace the lamp oil, and leave the key beside the bowl. I no longer dream of my mother waiting. I dream of the road, and the promise walking home without me. The debt was paid, but only after I became the one who carried it. What would you have done?
